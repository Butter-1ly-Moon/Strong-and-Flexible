What Are Biomechanics Sessions?

Biomechanics looks at how the body produces, absorbs and transfers forces during movement. A biomechanics session explores how different joints and body regions work together, how you organise movement and where compensations or inefficient strategies may be developing.

I do not assess the body as a collection of isolated parts. Instead, I look at how movement through the feet, ankles, knees, hips, pelvis, spine and upper body interacts as you walk, move, train or perform.

Photo placeholder

Depending on your needs, I may explore:

  • Gait and weight transfer
  • Joint movement and coordination
  • Movement compensations
  • Mobility and control
  • Loading strategies
  • Balance and rotation
  • Movement efficiency
  • Strength and physical capacity
  • Proprioception and kinaesthetic awareness

For broader context, the NHS describes gait analysis as the study of how a person walks and how forces act on the joints during walking. Oxford University Hospitals also explains gait assessment as a way of measuring walking patterns and understanding movement through the lower body.

How A Biomechanics Session Works

Every session is individual. I adapt what I assess and how I coach according to your history, movement patterns and goals rather than following a fixed protocol.

Understand Your Movement History

I begin by discussing your current concerns, previous injuries, training background, lifestyle and what you want to achieve.

This gives important context. The way someone moves cannot always be understood by looking at a single joint or one moment in isolation.

Explore Your Movement Patterns

I observe how your body organises movement through tasks relevant to you. This may include walking, gait, balance, rotation, weight transfer or other movement patterns.

I look for compensations, restrictions and strategies that may influence efficiency, comfort or performance.

From Understanding to Feeling

Take the foot as an example. I might draw its mechanics on a whiteboard, demonstrate with my own foot and explain how pressure moves between the heel and the bases of the big and little toes.

You may understand the explanation immediately. Recognising and producing that movement yourself can take longer.

Where appropriate, I use wedges and gentle tactile cueing, hands-on guidance, to help you explore what happens as you bend your knee and transfer weight. We might focus on how the arch responds to loading, or how the foot and lower leg move together.

The aim is to give you feedback you can recognise, rather than another instruction to memorise. Sometimes that recognition comes quickly. Sometimes it develops over several sessions: “I can actually feel my foot moving now.”

Build Capacity & Resilience

Depending on your goals, biomechanics work may be integrated with strength and conditioning principles, progressive loading, eccentric loading, neuromuscular re-education and overcoming isometrics.

The aim is to help you develop movement strategies that are adaptable, practical and relevant to the demands you place on your body.

Sometimes Less Movement Reveals More

People often move quickly or push further because they are looking for a strong sensation. But the detail we are exploring may be small, such as a shift in pressure, movement through a joint, or the point at which another part of the body takes over.

I recognise that impulse in myself. I’m hypermobile, and my instinct has often been to try harder and get there faster. Learning to slow down helped me notice details I was missing.

In sessions, we sometimes slow movement down to “increase the resolution”. That gives you time to notice what happens between the starting position and the endpoint, before exploring how it carries into everyday movement or training.

We Explore, Retest & Adapt

Before an exercise, I may ask you to notice how you stand, where you feel pressure beneath your feet, or how walking feels. Afterwards, we return to the same task and compare.

Your feedback matters alongside what I observe. You might notice clearer contact with the floor, easier movement or a change in discomfort. An immediate difference is useful information, but it does not necessarily mean lasting improvement.

If progress stalls, we reassess. That may mean changing the exercise, adjusting the demands or looking beyond the area we initially focused on. Where another form of assessment or care is needed, I will recommend it.

Biomechanics Sessions For Recovery & Performance

Biomechanics is not only relevant when something hurts.

For rehabilitation-focused clients, sessions can help build a clearer understanding of movement habits, compensations and loading strategies that may be associated with recurring issues.

For athletes and active professionals, the same process can be used to explore movement efficiency, mobility, coordination, strength and how the body responds to sporting or training demands.

The objective is not to force everyone towards a supposedly perfect movement pattern. It is to help you develop greater awareness, useful movement options and the physical capacity to perform with more confidence and resilience.

Frequently Asked Questions

What Does Biomechanics Mean?

Biomechanics is the study of movement and the forces acting on the body. In my sessions, I use biomechanics to explore how different areas of your body interact during walking, exercise, sport and other movement tasks.

Is Gait Analysis Always Included?

Gait analysis is an important part of my wider movement approach, but every session is tailored to the individual. I use the methods that are most relevant to your movement patterns and goals.

Are Sessions Only For Injuries?

No. Biomechanics sessions can also support people who want to improve movement efficiency, mobility, coordination, strength, athletic performance and long-term resilience.

Can Sessions Support Hypermobility?

Yes. My approach can be particularly useful for hypermobile individuals who want to improve movement awareness, control, strength and confidence rather than focusing only on increasing flexibility.

What Happens After My Session?

Your next steps depend on what the session reveals and what you want to achieve. This may involve continued biomechanics work, one-to-one movement coaching, strength development or other personalised rehabilitation and performance strategies.

The Goal Is Greater Independence

What I’m really looking for is the moment someone says, “Ah, I can actually feel it.”

An instruction becomes something you can recognise through your own experience, whether that is pressure beneath your feet, a shift in your weight, or different parts of your body moving together.

Demonstrations, hands-on guidance and wedges can help you find that awareness. Over time, the aim is to rely less on external cues and become more confident practising and adapting movement yourself.

Whether your goal is more comfortable everyday movement or greater confidence in training, I want you to leave with a clearer understanding of your body and practical ways to keep progressing.

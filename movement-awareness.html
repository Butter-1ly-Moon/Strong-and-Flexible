Movement Awareness:
Learning to Feel the Connections

How we notice movement, develop control and learn to use our bodies differently.

By Irek Bialek · 9 min read

Sometimes learning movement reminds me of looking at a picture. You know there is something there, but you cannot quite make it out. Someone points to a red triangle, and suddenly you see it clearly. You wonder how you missed it.

I see something similar when I work with people.

A small change in position, some resistance or a touch gives them something recognisable. “Oh, now I can feel it.” They notice a part of the foot contacting the floor, a rotation they had been missing, or how changing one movement affects another.

Those moments are a large part of why movement awareness interests me. Someone begins to recognise connections in their own body and has something they can explore for themselves.

What do I mean by movement awareness?

“Movement awareness” is a useful umbrella term. It includes several related abilities, and people can be good at some while finding others difficult.

There is the sense of where your limbs and joints are, how they are moving, and how much force or effort you are producing. These are commonly discussed within proprioception. Kinaesthesia refers more specifically to the sense of movement. A major review by Proske and Gandevia describes these different senses and how they contribute to our experience of the body.

In a session, the questions are usually more practical.

Can you feel where your weight is beneath your feet? Can you change the amount of effort you use? Can you move your pelvis without taking your whole upper body with it—and then bring those movements together when the task requires it?

Feeling a movement and controlling it are related, but they are different things. You might notice exactly what is happening and still struggle to change it. You might also perform a familiar movement very well without being able to describe much about it.

That is why I find broad labels such as “good body awareness” a little limited. I want to understand what someone can feel and do in the particular task in front of us.

Why noticing matters

Imagine trying to adjust a shower without being able to feel the water temperature. You can turn the control, but you have limited information about whether your adjustment is useful.

Movement is more complicated than a shower. We also rely on prediction, previous experience and responses that happen without conscious thought. But the analogy captures something important: feedback helps us judge and refine what we do.

Sometimes a client tells me, “I can feel that I’m not doing it.”

I consider that a useful starting point. They have recognised a difference between what they intended and what happened. We can make the task smaller, change the support or try another cue.

The person who cannot yet produce a movement has not necessarily failed to learn anything. Recognising the difficulty may be the first useful discovery.

Finding a cue that makes sense

Some people need to see a movement. They watch, try it and find their way into it.

Others understand the demonstration but cannot translate it into their own movement. Watching more closely does not always solve that problem.

In my experience, touch and resistance are often particularly useful. A hand can provide direction, a point of contact or something to push against. Resistance can make a movement more recognisable and give the person a response they can adjust.

I also receive information through that contact: how the person responds, where the effort changes and whether the task seems manageable.

There is no single cue that works for everyone. I might change the words, the position, the amount of resistance or the movement itself. A cue that made perfect sense yesterday may be less useful in a different task today.

The aim is to help the person recognise something they can increasingly find without me.

Connections between the foot and the rest of the body

One example I have noticed with a few clients concerns contact beneath the first metatarsal head—the ball of the foot behind the big toe.

During a foot drill or a movement such as a Jefferson curl, someone may tend to load the outside of the foot and struggle to maintain contact beneath that area.

Sometimes working directly with the foot helps. I might use my hands to provide resistance as the person explores turning or rolling the foot in different directions. When they stand again, I may observe a small change, and they may report that the movement or contact feels clearer.

With some clients, I have also explored an isometric hip exercise, then returned to the original task. Afterwards, they have reported more pressure beneath the ball of the big toe and a greater sense of stability there.

I do not know the exact mechanism behind those responses. The observation alone cannot tell me that a muscle was previously “switched off”, or that the hip caused the original difficulty.

What interests me is that a change in one task is followed by a recognisable difference in another. That gives us a relationship to investigate. Can the person find it again? Does it help the movement they care about? Is it still useful later?

When someone begins to notice those relationships themselves, the session becomes a shared investigation.

Being skilled in sport does not tell us everything

I work with people who are highly effective in their sport but find unfamiliar movement tasks surprisingly difficult. Others recognise subtle changes very quickly.

Neither response tells me everything about their athletic ability.

A boxer may have excellent timing, distance judgement and coordination, yet struggle to move the pelvis and ribcage separately in a slow exercise. That exercise asks a different question from boxing.

My own experience of martial arts makes this distinction easy to understand. I have generally found the visual timing of striking more difficult. Once I am in contact with someone while grappling, I find their shifts in weight and direction easier to read. Contact gives me information I can use.

Both situations involve skilled perception and movement, but the information and demands differ.

I often notice that people with varied movement experience have useful ways into learning something unfamiliar. I regard that as a coaching observation, rather than a rule. Experience can carry over, but we still need to practise the demands of the activity we want to improve.

A systematic review of sports-performance research found benefits from programmes described as proprioceptive training across several measures. The programmes and outcomes varied, so it would be too strong to say that developing one general quality called awareness improves every sporting skill.

Learning to feel can be part of learning to move

There is research behind the idea that sensory learning contributes to movement learning.

In an experiment by Darainy, Vahdat and Ostry, participants practised judging the direction of robot-guided arm movements and received feedback about their answers. This perceptual training improved their learning in a subsequent movement-adaptation task, with benefits lasting at least 24 hours.

That experiment did not test the kind of whole-body coaching I do. It does, however, provide an example of learning to distinguish sensory information helping subsequent movement learning.

Research on musicians offers another useful illustration of expertise. A study comparing pianists with non-musicians found faster and more independent finger movements in the pianists, without corresponding group differences in measured finger range or passive mechanical coupling.

These findings help explain why I am interested in what practice allows someone to perceive and control, alongside how strong or flexible they are.

Exploring slowly, then returning to the task

I sometimes work slowly with someone, asking them to notice pressure, rotation or the relationship between two movements.

That does not mean I want them thinking about every joint while playing a match or throwing a punch.

Research on attentional focus generally finds advantages when people attend to the intended effect of a movement, rather than closely monitoring their body parts during performance and learning. A large review by Chua and colleagues examines this distinction.

My practical approach is to explore something, then return to the activity and see whether the exploration was useful.

A young fighter I worked with responded strongly to a session exploring coordinated pelvic and ribcage movement, shoulder movement and pressure beneath his feet. Afterwards, he showed me his left hook and described feeling the rotation and sequence more clearly.

That was a meaningful response from him. It was not a measurement of punching power or proof of a permanent change. The longer-term question is whether he can use that discovery during training.

The exploration earns its place through what it helps the person do.

What about injury prevention?

I suspect that being able to notice and adapt movement can help people manage physical demands. That belief comes partly from my experience, and I would not present it as proof that more aware people always sustain fewer injuries.

There is stronger evidence for particular training programmes and outcomes.

In a trial involving 522 athletes after an ankle sprain, an eight-week home proprioceptive programme was added to usual care. Over the following year, 22% of the training group reported another sprain, compared with 33% of the usual-care group.

That is a useful result. It supports that programme in that setting. It cannot tell us that noticing more sensations protects every joint from injury.

Pain research also needs careful interpretation. A small study by Tsao, Danneels and Hodges found greater overlap in the brain’s motor representations of different back muscles in people with recurrent low-back pain. It used brain-stimulation mapping, not MRI. The association does not establish whether the changes preceded the pain, and I cannot infer someone’s brain organisation by watching them move.

For me, these studies provide reasons to investigate movement and learning carefully, while keeping the individual response central.

You do not have to start out good at it

I was not naturally good at every aspect of coordination. Learning to ride a bike took me a long time. At school, other children could keep a football in the air far more easily than I could.

The movement I can do now developed through years of practice, curiosity and investigation. My current ability does not describe where I started.

Research supports the possibility of change. A systematic review of proprioceptive learning found evidence of improvement with training, retention and some transfer between assessments. The details depend on what is practised and measured, but the encouraging point is that these abilities can develop.

You do not need exceptional flexibility or a sporting background to begin. You can explore and develop control within the movement available to you.

Sometimes the first step is a clearer contact beneath your foot. Sometimes it is using less effort. Sometimes it is simply recognising that two movements are difficult to separate.

Those discoveries give us somewhere to start. What I enjoy most is seeing someone become curious about them—and gradually learn to find the connections for themselves.

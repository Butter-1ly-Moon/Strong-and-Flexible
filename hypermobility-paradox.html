The Hypermobility Paradox:
Why Can I Be Flexible and Still Feel Tight?

You can have a large range of movement and still feel stiff, tense or restricted elsewhere. Research and practical experience help explain how flexibility, strength and movement awareness fit together.

By Irek Bialek · 8 min read

You can put your palms on the floor, move easily into a deep stretch, and still spend much of the day feeling tight. Or you may be very mobile in some places and surprisingly restricted elsewhere.

Perhaps stretching offers relief, but the feeling returns. Perhaps you enjoy yoga because certain positions feel accessible, yet balancing, controlling a small movement or knowing exactly where your weight is can be difficult.

I recognise these questions from my own body and from working with clients. Available range, the sensation of tightness and the ability to control movement tell us different things.

“Why am I loose here, but stiff somewhere else?”

This question often comes from people whose hypermobility has already been identified by another professional. They can see that some joints move a long way, but the label doesn’t seem to explain the areas that barely move.

One pattern I see is someone who can open their hips towards a lotus position with little practice, yet cannot reach the floor in a forward bend. Their ankles may have limited movement and their upper back may contribute very little to the tasks we explore.

Those are different movements, involving different joints and directions. Easy hip rotation does not tell us everything about hip flexion, ankle movement or how movement is distributed through the spine.

A small study of 32 women found that participants with asymptomatic generalised joint hypermobility did not have significantly greater quadriceps or hamstring extensibility than controls. Their measured knee position and force sense were also preserved. The findings help illustrate why a hypermobility label cannot describe every aspect of someone’s movement. Chaiparinya and colleagues, 2024.

When someone has spent decades sitting and uses relatively little variety of movement, that history belongs in the assessment. I look at it alongside previous injuries, symptoms, activity and the movement we can observe. The history alone does not establish the cause of a particular restriction.

A spectrum, with different individual profiles

Hypermobility describes joints that move beyond the usual range. It may affect a few joints or be more widespread, and it can exist without symptoms. A hypermobile joint is not necessarily unstable.

It is useful to think about the distribution of mobility and the impact on the person, rather than place everybody on a single scale from “a little flexible” to “extremely bendy”. Hypermobility spectrum disorders (HSD) and hypermobile Ehlers–Danlos syndrome (hEDS) involve clinical criteria and symptoms beyond range alone. HSD can also have a considerable impact on daily life. Castori and colleagues’ classification framework explains these distinctions.

How can loose joints coexist with tight muscles?

A useful distinction is between the movement a joint allows and how the body manages that movement.

Ligaments and joint capsules contribute to stability, while muscles help support and control the joint during activity. Where stability is difficult, greater muscular effort or co-contraction—muscles working together around a joint—may be part of the response.

A pilot study of 16 people found altered muscle activation in the hypermobile group, including greater co-contraction between certain thigh muscles during quiet standing. It supports investigating different stabilising strategies, rather than assuming everybody uses the same one. Greenwood and colleagues, 2011.

This offers a plausible explanation for part of the paradox: plenty of available joint movement can coexist with considerable muscular effort. But a tight sensation does not establish protective guarding, and a restricted area is not necessarily a required counterbalance to a loose one. Pain, fatigue, previous injury and local restrictions can also influence the picture.

Why stretching can feel so appealing

In my experience, the urge to stretch can be strong. A stretch provides a clear sensation and may feel relieving, even when the same area feels tight again later.

I understand why people return to something that offers relief. The useful question is what happens afterwards: does movement become easier and more comfortable, or are you repeatedly chasing the same sensation?

For some clients, I explore whether strength, support and control need more attention alongside flexibility. We might try a supported position, a controlled movement or resistance, then compare how the original task feels.

I teach yoga myself, and I can see the appeal of accessible, expansive positions. I also ask what happens on the way into and out of a pose. Can you slow down, change direction or maintain control before reaching your familiar endpoint? That gives us more information than the depth of the pose alone.

“I feel everything—how can my body awareness be poor?”

I meet people who notice sensations intensely but struggle to locate a movement or reproduce a position. Sensation can be strong without providing precise information for the task in front of you.

“Body awareness” is an umbrella term. In practice, we may be talking about:

  • Joint position sense: recognising where a joint or limb is.
  • Movement sense: noticing its direction and movement.
  • Force sense and force scaling: judging effort and adjusting how much force you produce.
  • Perceived pressure: noticing contact beneath your feet or hands.
  • Selective motor control: producing a particular movement without unnecessary movement elsewhere.

These involve overlapping sensory and motor processes. They are not a single ability that someone either possesses or lacks. A major review by Proske and Gandevia describes the different proprioceptive senses, including position, movement, effort and force.

In a small study involving nine people with Ehlers–Danlos syndrome, participants were less consistent than controls when locating their unseen hand. Their average accuracy was similar, but their responses were more scattered. This concerned a specific task and population, rather than every aspect of awareness in every hypermobile person. Clayton, Jones and Henriques, 2015.

Feeling a stretch strongly therefore does not, by itself, tell us how precisely someone can sense position or control movement. I explore this in more detail in Movement Awareness: Learning to Feel the Connections.

The wider nervous-system picture

Research in people with symptomatic hypermobility and EDS has documented autonomic difficulties and fatigue, sleep problems, and relationships between hypermobility and anxiety, including differences in brain activity during emotional processing.

These findings make the wider context relevant: how someone sleeps, recovers, experiences symptoms and responds to the demands of daily life. The studies do not establish one universal chain from loose joints to a constantly heightened state of alertness. Several processes may interact, and their importance will differ between people.

In practice, I want to understand how much effort movement takes for you, how manageable it feels, and how you respond during and after a session.

Hypermobility and athletic ability

Some hypermobile people are highly athletic. Others remember feeling awkward, repeatedly injured or convinced they were simply bad at sport.

My own story includes having to learn coordination and rebuild movement. Much of what people see me doing now developed through years of practice and investigation. My current ability does not describe where I started.

Hypermobility exists within high-level sport. Research has documented it in professional footballers and elite ballet dancers. In the ballet study, professionals demonstrated greater mobility in several lower-limb joints than the pre-professional group.

These findings show that hypermobility is compatible with demanding athletic performance; they do not prove that it causes sporting success. A larger available range may offer useful possibilities for a particular activity. Developing the strength, coordination and tolerance to use it is a further task.

When I think about dancing, swimming or martial arts, the useful question is which ranges and movement skills the activity demands. Someone’s injury history, symptoms, training opportunities and experience also matter. Flexibility alone cannot predict their athletic future.

What does training research tell us?

An encouraging study examined balance training in people with chronic ankle instability, comparing those with and without generalised joint hypermobility.

Forty people entered a 12-week supervised programme, and 35 completed the final follow-up. Both groups improved. The hypermobile group achieved comparable or better results on several measures, with benefits present three months after training. Certain ankle-strength improvements were also maintained more consistently in that group. Hou and colleagues, 2023.

This supports the possibility of a good response to structured rehabilitation. It does not establish that hypermobile people generally retain every kind of training gain for longer.

Learning is possible too. In a laboratory experiment, people with EDS adapted their sensed hand position after altered visual feedback to the same extent as controls. That supports an ability to adapt in the tested task, rather than a general claim of greater neuroplasticity or faster learning. Clayton, Cressman and Henriques, 2013.

A randomised trial involving 100 people with HSD and persistent shoulder symptoms compared higher-load and lower-load exercise programmes over 16 weeks. Higher-load training produced a statistically greater improvement in reported shoulder function, although the average difference was below the study’s stated threshold for clinical importance. Minor adverse effects, including muscle soreness, were more common. Liaghat and colleagues, 2022.

Strengthening deserves consideration, with the load, range and progression chosen for the individual.

What I look for in practice

I want to understand what you can do with the movement you already have, and where your options are limited.

Can you feel where you are? Can you manage the load? Can you move through a useful range, return from it and repeat the task with confidence?

Sometimes we begin with small, supported movements and clear feedback. Sometimes the useful work is progressively demanding strength training. We review your response, including how you feel later, and adapt.

My aim is to help you develop movement options you can use: in everyday life, in yoga, under a weight or in your sport. For someone who repeatedly feels tight despite being very flexible, that can provide a useful direction to investigate.
